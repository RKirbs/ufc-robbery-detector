The UFC Robbery Detector

Which UFC decisions did the statistics say went to the wrong fighter?

Every UFC fight that goes to the judges ends with three scorecards, and sometimes a lot of arguing. This project asks a narrower, answerable question: given everything the statistics recorded in each round, how likely was the declared winner to win? When that probability is very low, the stats and the judges strongly disagree. That’s what we call a robbery.

NoteWhat “robbery” means here

A robbery is a decision in which the officially declared winner had a low probability of winning given the round-by-round statistics of the fight, as estimated by a model trained on how judges actually score rounds.

Robbery Score = 1 − P(declared winner wins the decision | fight stats). 0 means the stats fully support the result, and values near 1 mean the stats strongly favoured the fighter who lost.

It is never an accusation of misconduct. Statistics can’t see everything a judge sees: damage, power, octagon control and aggression. A high score means “the numbers disagree”, not “the judges cheated”.

Igor Pokrajac def. Fabio Maldonado (May 2012) has the highest Robbery Score of all 4,049 decisions: 0.998. Model favored Maldonado in 3 of 3 rounds; Maldonado out-landed Pokrajac 98-36 in sig. strikes (ahead in 3 of 3); Pokrajac had 1 takedown to 0; Pokrajac had 3:20 more control time.

Headline findings

1 in 18 decisions is a statistical “robbery”. Of 4,049 UFC decisions from 2001 to 2026, 225 (5.6%) have a Robbery Score of at least 0.75. Decisions people still argue about land near the top: 11 of 13 widely debated decisions are in the top 16%. See the leaderboard →

What judges reward: a knockdown is worth about 12 significant head strikes, a minute of control about 3.1, and a takedown about 2.2. A body strike counts 0.75 of a head strike and a leg strike 0.59. Shooting for a takedown and missing costs a fighter (-0.67). See the methodology →

The model agrees with the media almost as often as the judges do. Where most media scorecards agreed, the official result matched them 90% of the time and the stats-only model 88%. The Robbery Score correlates with the share of media scoring the fight for the loser at r = 0.59. See the validation →

Judges lean slightly toward the red corner and the betting favourite. On a round the stats call 50/50, judges give it to the red corner +4.7 percentage points more often than the stats alone predict, and to a −300 betting favourite +6.5 points more often. Individual judges, adjusted for how close their rounds were, agree with the stats at very similar rates. See the judges →

The 2017 judging criteria changed what judges reward, but not how often decisions go “wrong”. A knockdown went from about 9 to 15 head strikes and a takedown from 2.7 to 1.9. 10-8 rounds peaked at 6.0% in 2017 and have since faded. Robbery rates show no clear change. See the 2017 analysis →

How it works, in one paragraph

Round-by-round statistics come from UFCStats, and every judge’s round-by-round scorecard plus media scorecards come from MMADecisions (4,109 decisions, 33,444 judge-round scores, 51,355 media scores). A model learns how judges turn a round’s stats into a 10-9, using only that round’s numbers and treating both corners identically. The model’s round probabilities feed a simulation of 10,000 sets of three correlated judges for every fight, which gives the probability that each fighter wins the decision. Full methodology →
